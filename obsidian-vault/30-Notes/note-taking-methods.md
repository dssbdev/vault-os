# Note-Taking Methods

## Overview

Three well-known systems for organising notes, in plain language, plus the one idea that matters more than any of them: folders, links, and tags answer three different questions, and most systems get into trouble by making all three do the same job.

## Key concepts

**The three tools.** A folder answers *where does this live* - one home per note, chosen by lifecycle. A link answers *what does this connect to* - cheap, unlimited, and it works in both directions, so every link makes two notes easier to find. A tag answers *what kind of thing is this* - a property, not a place. Trouble starts when tags become a second folder system or when folders are asked to express meaning, which they can't, because a note only gets one folder and ideas belong to several topics at once.

**Zettelkasten** - small notes, one idea each, densely linked, structure emerging from the links rather than imposed up front. Built by and for people who write for a living. Strength: it produces connections you didn't plan. Cost: high maintenance, and hundreds of fragments become a click-safari when you just want to remember how something worked.

**PARA** - four buckets sorted by how soon you'll act. Projects have an end date, Areas are ongoing responsibilities, Resources are reference material, Archive is everything finished. Strength: brutally practical, and the Projects-versus-Areas distinction alone fixes most people's todo lists. Cost: it organises by action, so pure knowledge notes sit awkwardly.

**Maps of content** - an index note that curates links to a topic, written by hand. Useful once a topic outgrows its folder, useless before that, because an index of three notes is just a shorter list.

**Note size.** Orthodoxy says split everything into atoms. The opposite position, and the one this vault takes: fewer, larger, well-structured notes, with headings as the internal boundary. A cold read six months later wants the context in one place, not scattered across nine files. If a machine ever needs to search these notes, it can split them at the headings itself - you get the small pieces without having to maintain them.

## Practical application

Steal, don't convert. Take PARA's lifecycle folders because they answer "where does this go" fastest, take the linking discipline from Zettelkasten because links are what make a pile of notes into a system, and add an index note only when a folder visibly overflows. The test for any convention is whether it survives a busy week - the systems people abandon are the ones that demand upkeep on the days they most need the notes.
